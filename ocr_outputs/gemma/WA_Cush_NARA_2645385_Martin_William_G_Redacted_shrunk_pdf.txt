Tacoma, Wash., May 16, 1919

Mrs. E. F. Biggs
4619 Woodland Park Ave.
Seattle, Wash.

Dear Mrs. Briggs:

William has made request for vacation leave. He has been doing very well at school and I do not think it advisable to grant him leave for the full vacation period, as he will fall into habits that will cause trouble for himself and the school on re-entering next fall.

During the vacation period, half the day will be given to industrial training and credit will be given him in his school work; the other half day, he may obtain work on the outside and, it may be, I shall be able to place him on the pay roll for a short time.

Very respectfully,

Superintendent.

Mr C. V. Peel
Dear Sir
I just received your letter this evening saying that my son William Martin got into trouble by breaking into a store. I am grief stricken over it. It seems as tho my troubles will never be over in this world. When did he get into this trouble and when did the hearing come off. and when was he sentenced and taken to monroe. [Right Page - Typed Letter]
Cushman Indian School
Tacoma, Wash., April 23, 1920

Mrs. E. F. Biggs
South Colby, Washington

Dear Madam:

I have your letter of April 16 making further inquiries about your son's trouble. I sympathize with you in the burden of sorrow you feel.

It appears that a number of thefts committed by some of the boys at this school were reported to the Tacoma police and that several boys were taken by the police in the early part of the year. These boys admitted the thefts and were excused on the promise to do no more stealing. But about a month ago, the owner of a store near the school, complained to the police that some of the Indian boys from this school had broken into the store and had stolen a quantity of goods.

The police located the boys who admitted to the theft, and took them before the juvenile court in Tacoma. Three of the boys, one of whom was your boy, were given sentences from one to fifteen years each in the State Reform School. Your son and another boy, being over 18 years of age, were sent to Monroe, Washington.

I suggest that you write your boy an encouraging letter and ask him to obey all the rules of the Reformatory and by so doing he may be able to get released in the shortest possible time. Otherwise, if he is refractory, he may be required to serve out the full sentence.

I regret very much that these boys were guilty of the violation of the law, as this institution has constantly urged upon the pupils the necessity of obeying not only the rules of the school, but the laws of the State.

Very respectfully,

Special Agent in Charge.
